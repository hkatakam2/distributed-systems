Lesson 0017

Distributed Coordination: Leaders, Leases, and Fencing

The seventeenth mental model: coordination is not a magic lock. It is a correctness boundary for deciding who may act, for how long, and how stale actors are prevented from causing side effects.

Mission link: this is the missing layer behind safe repair jobs, shard ownership, stream processors, schedulers, rollout controllers, and any resume system that claims one active owner.

Where This Fits

Lesson 0016 taught how to review a production architecture as claims, failure modes, controls, evidence, and owners. This lesson focuses on one high-risk claim: "only one worker is allowed to do this right now."

Keep Reference 0017: Distributed Coordination nearby. It is the compact checklist for leader election, leases, and fencing.

Concise Answers From Lesson 0016

  1. Review claims, not boxes: a diagram becomes useful when each component supports a testable guarantee.
  2. Name the invariant: every important claim needs the condition that must remain true under retry, replay, failover, and rollout.
  3. Attach controls to failures: duplicate commands, stuck outbox rows, hot partitions, stale caches, unsafe schema changes, and runaway backfills each need a named control.
  4. Evidence matters: production readiness requires signals that distinguish likely causes, not just dashboards that prove a component exists.
  5. Strong defenses have shape: user promise, mechanism, tradeoff, metric, and recovery path.

The Big Idea

Distributed coordination is needed when independent processes must agree on a small piece of control-plane truth: who is leader, which worker owns a shard, whether a migration may start, or what command was chosen at a particular position in a replicated log.

The Raft consensus project is the clean entry point for the consensus model: a cluster of machines agrees on ordered decisions despite machine failures. The Chubby paper shows the production shape at Google: a highly available lock service used for coarse-grained coordination and low-volume metadata, not as a hot data path. ZooKeeper recipes and the etcd concurrency API show the same practical primitives: sessions or leases, ordered keys, locks, elections, and ownership checks.

Coordination loop:

need one actor
  -> acquire session or lease
  -> receive epoch or fencing token
  -> perform side effects only if token is accepted
  -> renew or lose ownership
  -> expose churn, conflicts, and stale-token rejections

The dangerous misunderstanding is thinking the lock itself prevents all harm. A lease can expire while the old holder is paused. The old holder can later wake up and try to write. That is why fencing tokens matter: every new owner gets a higher token, and the downstream resource rejects writes from older tokens.

Scenario: Projection Repair Coordinator

Suppose your billing pipeline discovers bad derived revenue state for some merchants. You need a repair job that replays Kafka events into PostgreSQL projection tables and refreshes Redis serving keys. Multiple Kubernetes pods are running because you want failover, but only one coordinator should assign repair ranges at a time.

PodsSeveral candidates can run.
LeaseOne candidate owns coordination.
TokenEach ownership term has an epoch.
WorkersRanges are processed idempotently.
SinksWrites reject stale tokens.

If the active coordinator crashes, another pod should take over. If the active coordinator pauses for 60 seconds and loses its lease, it must not later overwrite repair state assigned by the new coordinator. That second sentence is the part many designs miss.

Step 1: Decide If Coordination Is Actually Needed

First classify the risk. A cheap lock may be fine when duplicate work merely wastes CPU. It is not enough when duplicate work breaks a durable invariant or triggers external side effects.

Need Example Usual answer
Efficiency only Do not run the same cache warmer twice. Best-effort lock or idempotent duplicate work is often acceptable.
Short critical section Claim one pending job row. Database transaction, row lock, or atomic update close to the data.
Long ownership Own a stream shard, scheduler, or repair coordinator. Lease or session with heartbeat, plus fencing on side effects.
Replicated decision Choose the next command in a replicated metadata log. Consensus-backed service such as etcd, ZooKeeper, or a Raft-based component.

One strong heuristic: if a stale owner can corrupt state after it loses ownership, you need fencing or an equivalent invariant at the resource being modified.

Step 2: Separate Lease From Fencing

A lease answers "who currently appears to own this?" A fencing token answers "is this actor newer than the last actor this resource accepted?" Both are needed for long-running correctness-sensitive work.

Lease: time-bounded ownership maintained by session, heartbeat, or TTL. It helps new owners take over when the old owner fails.

Fencing token: monotonically increasing ownership number. Every side effect includes the token, and the resource rejects older tokens.

Resource check: database row version, compare-and-set, conditional update, idempotency key, stream generation, or storage precondition that enforces the token.

Stale-owner failure:

coordinator A gets lease with token 7
A pauses before writing repair assignment
lease expires
coordinator B gets lease with token 8
B writes assignment with token 8
A resumes and tries assignment with token 7
projection_repair table rejects token 7

Without the final rejection, the system merely hopes that lease timing and process scheduling line up. Hope is not a correctness mechanism.

Step 3: Put Coordination in the Control Plane

Coordination services are deliberately small and careful. Use them to decide ownership, store small metadata, elect leaders, or publish configuration versions. Do not use them as the high-throughput event stream, metrics store, or blob store.

Primitive Good fit Common mistake
Kafka consumer group Partition ownership and failover for stream consumption. Treating it as proof that every downstream write is fenced.
PostgreSQL row claim Claiming bounded jobs close to relational state. Holding a transaction open around long external work.
etcd or ZooKeeper Leader election, leases, ordered metadata, service coordination. Putting hot business events or large payloads through the coordination path.
Redis lock Reducing duplicate work when failure is tolerable. Using lock ownership as the only guard for correctness-sensitive side effects.

Step 4: Make the Ownership Claim Reviewable

Use the review loop from Lesson 0016. "We use leader election" is a tool statement. The production claim is more precise.

Review item Projection repair answer
Claim At most one live coordinator can assign repair ranges accepted by sinks.
Mechanism Consensus-backed lease grants ownership token; sinks use conditional writes.
Invariant No sink accepts a write from a token lower than the latest accepted token for that repair job.
Failure mode Old coordinator pauses, loses lease, resumes, and attempts stale writes.
Control Fencing token on every repair assignment and projection write.
Evidence Leadership churn, lease-renewal failures, stale-token rejection count, duplicate assignment attempts.
Owner The team owning the repair controller also owns the lease runbook and stale-token alerts.

Step 5: Defend the Design

When challenged, do not say "we used etcd so only one pod runs." That skips the actual safety argument.

Weak answer: "We added a distributed lock so the repair job is safe."

Strong answer: "The repair coordinator uses a consensus-backed lease for failover, but we do not rely on the lease alone. Each ownership term receives a monotonically increasing token. Every repair assignment and sink update includes that token, and PostgreSQL rejects stale tokens with conditional updates. If a coordinator pauses and resumes after losing ownership, its writes fail. We monitor lease churn, renewal failures, duplicate assignment attempts, and stale-token rejections."

The shape is the point: leader election chooses an owner, fencing protects the resource, and observability proves the ownership claim is holding.

Reflection Questions

No homework is required. These are the checks that show whether you can defend coordination instead of naming a lock library:

  1. For a repair coordinator, what is the difference between the lease and the fencing token?
  2. Why is "only one pod has the lock" not a complete correctness argument?
  3. Where would you enforce stale-token rejection in a PostgreSQL-backed projection repair?
  4. When would Kafka consumer group ownership be enough, and when would it not be enough?
  5. What evidence would you want before trusting the coordination design in production?

Concise answer key: the lease grants temporary ownership while the fencing token proves freshness to downstream resources; a lock alone does not stop paused old owners from writing late; PostgreSQL should enforce token checks with conditional updates or versioned rows near the projection state; consumer group ownership is enough for partition assignment but not for unfenced external side effects; production evidence should include leadership churn, renewal failures, stale-token rejections, duplicate attempts, and repair success rate.

Ask follow-up questions about any unclear piece. Useful review questions here are: "What is the invariant?", "What happens if the old leader resumes?", "Which resource enforces the token?", and "Is this coordination for correctness or just efficiency?"
